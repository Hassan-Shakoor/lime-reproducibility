For each of the 50 runs (2 models $\times$ 5 conditions $\times$ 5 seeds), I train a logistic regression and explain 30 injected test reviews with LIME ($K = 10$, 2,000 samples). I used two different feature representations to get models that depend on the artefact to very different degrees. \emph{Binary} bag-of-words means that a token is worth as much as any other word. In \emph{TF-IDF}, length normalisation makes the token’s weight very small, since it is 1 word out of about 700. The models are linear and so I can measure their true reliance: the drop in P(negative) when the token is deleted ($\Delta$P).

Table~\ref{tab:constructed} and Figure~\ref{fig:constructed} give the results. \textbf{The binary model reproduces the paper's 20 Newsgroups failure in a controlled form.} When every negative review carries the token, the model scores 99.6\% with it but 67.2\% once it is removed, the same kind of collapse as the original SVM's drop from the 20NG test split to the religion pages. LIME ranks the token \emph{first} in 100\% of these reviews, and still in 99.3\% when only a quarter of negative reviews carry it. In the control, where the token predicts nothing, LIME never lists it. \textbf{The TF-IDF model shows the converse.} The token is that model's largest or near-largest coefficient (largest at neg100 and neg75, third at neg25), yet deleting it changes a prediction by only 0.016, and accuracy with and without it differs by at most 2 points. This is again reflected in LIME: it rarely ranks the token first, but puts it in the top ten 69--75\% of the time, dropping to 43\% at the weakest condition. The weight assigned by LIME to the token tracks the measured reliance (Pearson $r = 0.98$ binary, $0.94$ TF-IDF; Figure~\ref{fig:constructed}c) across the 20 non-control runs per model. For TF-IDF the reliance is only 0.012--0.016, so the correlation mainly separates the weakest condition from the others (within neg100--neg50, $r = 0.64$).

These results support both claims on new data. LIME is faithful (C1) in that its weight depends on the extent to which the model makes use of the artefact, as opposed to how well the artefact correlates with the label in the data; after all, the two models have an identical label correlation. The method can also be used to spot untrustworthy models (C2): LIME puts the artefact at the top of the rankings if the model has a heavy dependence on it, such as in the case of binary neg100 where 32 accuracy points are forfeited in its absence. LIME tracks reliance rather than the accuracy cost, so it also ranks the token first at binary neg25, where removing it costs only 1.4 points; judging whether a flagged feature matters still needs a held-out check. The evaluation goes beyond the replication in one respect. The paper could only \emph{show} that header words appear in explanations; here the size of the spurious effect is known, so LIME's sensitivity can be measured as a function of it.
